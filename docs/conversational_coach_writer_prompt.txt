FULL COACHING KNOWLEDGE BASE:
# Fencing Lunge: Knowledge Base for an AI Fencing Coach

**Purpose:** Reference material for a coaching agent giving feedback on the fencing lunge (foil, épée, sabre).
**Compiled:** October 2026 from web research. All content is paraphrased from the sources listed at the end.
**Confidence labels:** `[HIGH]` = multiple independent sources agree. `[MED]` = one good source or minor disagreement. `[LOW]` = forum or single informal source; treat as a heuristic.

---

## 1. What the lunge is

The lunge is the most common attacking footwork action in modern fencing. The fencer extends the weapon arm toward the target, then launches the body forward from the en garde stance by kicking the front leg out and straightening the rear leg, finishing in a long, low position with the point on target. It must be followed by a controlled recovery back to en garde. `[HIGH]`

A good lunge is fast, smooth, covers distance without warning, keeps the point aimed throughout, and ends in balance so the fencer can recover or continue (redouble, remise, reprise).

---

## 2. Starting position (en garde prerequisites)

A lunge is only as good as the stance it starts from. `[HIGH]`

- Front foot points straight at the opponent. Rear foot is roughly perpendicular to it (about 90°).
- Both knees bent, weight balanced, ready to move in either direction.
- Weapon arm bent in front, elbow tucked in line with forearm, hand, guard and point. Rear arm held back and out of the way.
- Feet spacing varies by source and coach (from roughly shoulder width to about 3 ft). The agent should not give one exact number. Suggest the fencer confirm spacing with their coach. `[MED]`
- Knees and toes should track the same direction as the feet. One coach notes that foot and knee problems usually come from the hip and upper leg, so the fix is to direct the upper leg rather than only the toes or knee. `[MED]`

---

## 3. The correct sequence (core model)

**Coaching principle: the blade moves first and the body follows it. Short cue: "hand first, then feet."** `[HIGH]`

1. **Extend the weapon arm.**
   - Raise the hand to about shoulder height and straighten the arm toward the target, without locking the elbow.
   - Keep the weapon shoulder relaxed. Do not shrug or lift it with the neck muscles.
   - Elbow, forearm, guard and point stay in one line, parallel to the floor. Do not chop or drop the point.
2. **Kick the front foot out.**
   - Lift the toes and send the front leg forward low to the floor, leading with the heel.
   - The front leg straightens at the knee as it travels. Do not lean forward and use the front foot to spring off the floor (see Errors).
3. **Drive with the rear leg.**
   - The rear leg straightens and pushes the body toward the target.
   - Apply the push **down into the floor with the whole rear foot**, not backwards. One épée master teaches the image of pushing through the floorboards. `[MED]`
   - The rear arm swings back and down, in line with the weapon arm, to counterbalance.
4. **Land and finish.**
   - Front heel lands first, then the foot rolls flat. Front knee ends over the ankle/instep, not past the toes. `[HIGH]`
   - Rear leg ends straight, with a straight line from rear foot through knee and hip to shoulder.
   - Torso stays upright or only slightly forward. Excess forward lean is a flagged error (see Errors).
   - Point stays aimed at the target. The weapon arm does not drop on landing.
5. **Recover.** See Section 5.

**Timing of the arm relative to the legs** (from a UNC coaching drill set) `[MED]`

| Name | When the arm extends |
|---|---|
| Pre-initiative tempo | Fully extended before any movement (point in line) |
| Initiative tempo | Begins just before the footwork starts (common for long attacks) |
| Saved tempo | After the lunge has started |
| Lost tempo | After the lunge is finished (usually too late) |

The default teaching is arm first. Each tempo has tactical uses.

---

## 4. Key technical details

**Arm, aim and shoulder** `[MED]`
- A tight shoulder drops the arm during the lunge and ruins aim. Cue: make the shoulder "loose like water." A drill is to hold the arm extended and aimed while jumping up and down without moving the point.
- Use the dominant eye. Eye dominance and handedness don't always match, and a mismatch makes aiming harder.
- Watch the opponent's weapon shoulder to read arm movement and distance, and drop the eyes to a target only once committed.
- Make small aim corrections with the fingers, not the shoulder.

**Rear arm** `[HIGH]`
- Thrown back or down during the final motion for balance. A rear arm that doesn't go back is a listed error.
- Older Italian-tradition teaching sometimes brings the rear arm forward to protect the face and chest. The modern sport-fencing convention is to throw it back.

**Front leg** `[MED]`
- Toes are pointed up as the leg travels forward. Land heel first.
- Front shoulder, knee and foot align nearly vertically at full extension in thrust-fencing descriptions.
- Don't hyperextend the lead knee. A biomechanics study found some fencers hyperextended the lead leg on lunge and recovery.

**Rear foot**
- It stays on the floor and does not roll sideways at the end. `[MED]`
- In a long lunge the rear foot will usually slide forward slightly (rear-foot drag). One fencer's heuristic: if the rear foot doesn't move at all, you may be dropping down instead of travelling forward. `[LOW]`

**Distance beats depth** `[LOW, forum, but consistent with coaching principles]`
- Correct distance and timing matter more than a very long or deep lunge. Don't chase depth at the cost of speed, balance or control. Work on acceleration and travel.

**Sabre-specific constraint**
- Crossing the rear leg in front of the front leg during forward movement is not permitted in sabre. Sabre bouts often begin with immediate, explosive lunge-type attacks. `[MED]`

---

## 5. Recovery

Always include recovery in practice. `[HIGH]`

**Backward recovery** (most common)
1. Unlock and bend the rear knee.
2. Push back with the front leg and pull the body back with the rear leg together. One coach points out that once the front foot leaves the floor it cannot push, while the rear foot still has traction. Use the rear leg actively, bending and drawing it in. `[MED]`
3. Return to en garde balance.
4. **Retract the weapon arm last.** Pulling the arm back before the body has withdrawn leaves a large target for a counter-attack. `[HIGH]`

**Forward recovery:** pull the rear foot up into en garde, advancing instead of retreating. Used to continue pressure or follow with another attack. `[HIGH]`

---

## 6. Common errors → cause → fix

| Symptom | Likely cause | Suggested fix |
|---|---|---|
| Lunge is slow, short, or telegraphed | Leaning forward and springing off the front foot instead of kicking it out first | Drill the front-foot kick first with an upright torso, then add the rear drive `[MED]` |
| Point drops during the lunge | Tense or lifted weapon shoulder; poor elbow alignment | Relaxed-shoulder drill; check elbow is tucked in line with forearm and point `[MED]` |
| Knee ends past the front foot | Front foot not kicked far enough; dropping rather than travelling | Focus on front-leg travel and heel landing `[HIGH]` |
| Torso leans heavily forward | Compensating for balance or strength deficits | Strengthen core and legs; practice slow lunges with a stable torso `[HIGH]` |
| Rear arm doesn't go back | Habit or unfamiliarity | Cue the rear arm throw as part of the finish `[HIGH]` |
| Arm retracts before body in recovery | Wrong sequencing | Slow-motion recovery drills; arm last `[HIGH]` |
| Rear foot rolls sideways or lifts | Weak rear-leg mechanics | Keep the rear foot flat on the floor and push it down `[MED]` |
| Rear-leg power goes backward, not forward | Misunderstanding the push direction | Cue "push down into the floor" `[MED]` |
| Front knee collapses inward | Weak hip control, foot angle, or knee tracking | Check foot direction and hip/upper-leg alignment; strengthen hip stabilisers `[MED]` |
| Opponent always reads your lunge | Same starting position or visible preparation every time | Vary preparation and starting blade position; avoid leaning or tensing beforehand `[MED]` |
| Wild blade movement during the lunge | Over-correcting with the shoulder | Small finger adjustments only `[MED]` |

---

## 7. Weapon-specific notes

- **Foil / épée:** Both are taught the lunge in the same way, but a 2024 study of 16 foil and 16 épée fencers found different arm-leg and shoulder-hip coordination between them, which the authors tie to each weapon's rules. A small EMG study (8 subjects) found higher rectus femoris activity in foil lunges, and noted that épée fencers also train the fléche heavily. Treat both as suggestive, not definitive. `[LOW–MED]`
- **Épée:** Because the whole body is the target and hits are simultaneous, the lunge is often used with shorter, controlled attacks to the hand or forearm, plus second-intention and counter-time actions. Medium and short lunges and remise/reprise follow-ups feature in drills. `[MED]`
- **Foil:** Right-of-way rules mean the lunge is typically preceded by a clear threat or preparation (point in line, advance). `[MED]`
- **Sabre:** No crossovers when moving forward. The attack often starts immediately off the "fence" command. Distance management and speed dominate. `[MED]`

---

## 8. Variations

- **Advance-lunge:** a step forward followed by a lunge, to cover extra distance. Practice the rhythm so the rear foot lands before the lunge launches. `[HIGH]`
- **Lunge with forward recovery:** recover by pulling the rear foot up. Can be used as a "false" first attack that sets up a second lunge. `[MED]`
- **Firm-footed lunge** (historical Italian rapier): the front foot stays planted while the body extends. Mostly of historical interest in modern sport fencing. `[MED]`
- **Repeated lunge / redouble / remise / reprise:** follow-up actions after the first lunge, relying on good balance and quick recovery. `[HIGH]`
- **Fléche:** the running attack, an alternative to the lunge, with its own mechanics (the same "hand first, then feet" principle applies). `[MED]`

---

## 9. Drills

1. **Slow-motion lunge.** Perform the full sequence slowly with perfect form and recover. Speed comes later and feels more natural. `[MED]`
2. **Balance lunges.** Lunge and hold at full extension, engaging the core, then recover using the core and legs rather than the arms. Aim for five clean repetitions without losing balance. `[MED]`
3. **Lunge-recover-forward-lunge.** Lunge, recover forward by bringing the rear foot up fast, then lunge again at speed. Time it for speed and control. `[MED]`
4. **Lunging on the edge of the strip.** Use the strip edge to check the lunge goes straight. `[LOW]`
5. **Relaxed-shoulder jump drill.** Hold the arm extended on target and jump up and down without disturbing the point. `[MED]`
6. **Distance and tempo drills.** Incremental-distance lunges (short to longest), arm-extension tempo variations, advance-lunge option drills. `[MED]`
7. **Lunge to a target vs. no target.** A kinematics study found that lunging at a target changes the motor strategy and that the unarmed arm affects performance. Train both. `[MED]`
8. **Cool-down lunges.** Slow, medium-length lunges, exhaling on each lunge. `[LOW]`
9. **Warm-up progression.** Gradually increase lunge speed and distance in the warm-up; include hip and ankle mobility. `[MED]`

---

## 10. Physical preparation and injury notes

- **Power:** Explosive strength in the glutes, hips and posterior chain. Repeat-lunge ability is linked to lower-body power, reactive strength and speed (Swansea study). `[MED]`
- **Mobility:** Hips, hip flexors, hamstrings, groin, lower back and ankles. Flexibility helps but technique matters more than raw range. `[MED]`
- **Muscle imbalance:** Repeated lunging loads the lead and rear sides asymmetrically. A study of elite fencers used targeted training to reduce this imbalance. Suggest balanced strength work. `[MED]`
- **Knee safety:** Keep the front knee tracking in line with the front foot. Avoid inward knee collapse, and avoid landing hard. The impact of repeated lunges is a recognised cause of overuse injury. `[MED]` (Knee-tracking guidance comes from general strength-training literature and is adapted to fencing.)
- The agent should not diagnose injuries. If a fencer reports pain, recommend rest and a qualified professional.

---

## 11. Guidance for the coaching agent

- **Ask for observable details:** Which weapon? Which foot lands first? Where does the arm start? Is there video? A text description is a weak substitute for seeing the lunge.
- **Fix one thing at a time.** Prioritise: (1) arm-first sequencing, (2) front-foot kick vs. leaning, (3) balance and landing alignment, (4) recovery with arm last, (5) speed and distance.
- **Use short cues:** "Hand first, then feet." "Kick the foot out, don't lean." "Push the floor, not backwards." "Shoulder like water." "Arm back last."
- **Distinguish technique from tactics:** a technically perfect lunge at the wrong distance or time still fails.
- **Acknowledge variation:** coaches and schools differ on details like foot spacing and torso lean. Where sources conflict, say so and defer to the fencer's own coach.
- **Safety:** Do not push a fencer into depth or speed beyond their current mobility and strength.
