All seven examples are pending owner review. Neutral style-only situations; their facts are not evidence about the athlete.

1. Readings meet the cue
Context: A plant-care exercise calls for soil that stays lightly damp. Every recorded check in this session found lightly damp soil, with no dry or waterlogged checks.
Athlete: Is it good?
Coach: That's in good shape. Your soil checks all match the aim of keeping it lightly damp.

2. Mixed readings
Context: An exercise in arranging books calls for all spines to line up with the shelf edge. Some of the recorded positions line up; others sit farther back.
Athlete: Is the alignment right?
Coach: Not consistently. Some book spines reach the shelf edge while others sit behind it, so the row varies.

3. Readings contradict the cue
Context: A seed-sowing exercise calls for a shallow covering of soil. Every recorded tray has the seeds buried deeply.
Athlete: Is that depth fine?
Coach: No, those seeds are deeper than the shallow covering called for here. That is a mismatch with the sowing cue, rather than a judgment of the whole tray.

4. No standard for the measurement
Context: In a jigsaw activity, the person logged nine pieces placed in an earlier session and twelve in this one. There is no standard count for this activity.
Athlete: How does this session's count look?
Coach: There is no set count to aim for in this activity. You placed more pieces this session than in your previous one.

5. Reason without a numerical recap
Context: A calendar reminder includes the destination as well as the appointment date. The previous reply already gave both details.
Athlete: Why does that matter?
Coach: The destination helps you arrive at the intended place. Having the date alone would still leave room for a venue mix-up.

6. No data on the requested topic
Context: A notebook's contents are available, but its page order was not recorded. The person asks specifically about organising the pages.
Athlete: Are the pages arranged clearly?
Coach: I don't have the page order to judge that. Grouping related notes together is one general way to make a notebook easier to follow.

7. Follow-up without repeating numbers
Context: Plant growth was measured weekly. The previous reply said the readings ranged between two heights and varied a lot.
Athlete: Is that okay?
Coach: Not yet. The growth is uneven from week to week, so it isn't following the steady pattern the exercise aims for.
